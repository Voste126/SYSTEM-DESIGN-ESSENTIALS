interview prep — chapter 03

A framework for system design interviews

The design matters less than the process you used to get there. Four steps, a rough time budget, and the checklist worth internalizing before the next on-site.

01

Understand the problem and establish scope

The single biggest mistake in this step is answering fast without understanding the question — confidence isn't a substitute for clarity, and an interviewer reads "jumped straight to an answer" as a warning sign, not a strength. Slow down. Ask questions. If the interviewer can't answer directly, they'll ask you to state an assumption instead — write it down, you'll need it later.

A starting question template: What specific features are we building? How many users does the product have? How fast is the company expecting to scale — what's the load in 3, 6, 12 months? What's the existing tech stack, and what internal services could simplify this?

Worked example — a news feed system. The clarifying pass covers: mobile, web, or both (both); the two features that matter most (posting, viewing friends' feeds); whether the feed is strict reverse-chronological or ranked (kept simple: reverse-chronological); max friends per user (5,000); daily active users (10 million); and whether posts include media (yes — images and video).

02

Propose high-level design and get buy-in

Reach agreement with the interviewer on a blueprint, treating them as a collaborator rather than a silent judge. Sketch box diagrams with the key components — clients, APIs, web servers, data stores, cache, CDN, message queue. Run back-of-the-envelope math to check the blueprint actually fits the scale from step 1, and say out loud that you're about to do this before diving in. Walk through a few concrete use cases — this often surfaces edge cases you hadn't considered.

Whether to go down to API endpoints and schema detail depends on the problem's scope — too low-level for "design Google search," entirely fair game for "design the backend of a multiplayer poker game." When unsure, ask.

Worked example continued. The news feed splits into two flows: feed publishing (a new post writes to cache/database, then gets pushed into friends' feeds) and newsfeed building (aggregating friends' posts, sorted reverse-chronologically, at read time).

03

Design deep dive

By now you and the interviewer should agree on scope, have a blueprint, gotten feedback on it, and have a sense of where to go deeper. This varies a lot: sometimes the interviewer wants more breadth, sometimes (especially for senior candidates) performance characteristics and bottlenecks, and most often one or two specific components in real depth — the hash function for a URL shortener, or latency and presence tracking for a chat system.

Time management matters more here than anywhere else. It's easy to fall into a fascinating rabbit hole that doesn't actually demonstrate scalable-system-design ability — spending real interview time on the exact mechanics of a specific ranking algorithm burns the clock without proving the skill being assessed.

04

Wrap up

  • Identify bottlenecks in your own design and how you'd improve them — never claim it's perfect, that's an immediate credibility loss.
  • Recap the design, especially if you proposed multiple approaches during the session.
  • Error cases (server failure, network loss) are worth discussing.
  • Operational concerns: how would you monitor metrics and error logs, how would you roll this out?
  • The "next scale curve" is a favorite follow-up — if this supports 1M users, what changes to support 10M?
  • If time remains, propose additional refinements you'd make given more of it.

05

Dos and don'ts

DO

  • Always ask for clarification — never assume your assumption is correct.
  • Understand the actual requirements before proposing anything.
  • Accept there's no single right answer — startup-scale and millions-of-users solutions to the same prompt look completely different, and that's fine.
  • Say what you're thinking out loud, constantly.
  • Suggest multiple approaches where it makes sense.
  • Agree on the blueprint first, then drill into the most critical component first.
  • Bounce ideas off the interviewer — a good one is a teammate, not a judge.
  • Never give up, even when a component turns out harder than expected.

DON'T

  • Show up unprepared for the typical/common questions.
  • Jump into a solution before clarifying requirements and assumptions.
  • Go deep on one component too early — high-level first, then drill down.
  • Hesitate to ask for a hint if stuck.
  • Think in silence — communicate continuously.
  • Assume you're done once you've given a design — you're done when the interviewer says so.
