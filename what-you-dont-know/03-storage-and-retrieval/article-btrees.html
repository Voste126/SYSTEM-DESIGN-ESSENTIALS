B-trees: the most common index of all

Everything in part 1 was append-only. B-trees do the opposite — they overwrite fixed-size pages in place — and they're the default in almost every relational database. Press play to watch a lookup, then a page split, actually happen.

How a lookup actually traverses the tree

Start at the root. Each page holds keys that mark boundaries and references to child pages responsible for the range between those boundaries. Follow the right reference at each level until you reach a leaf page holding the actual value.

Root page first, then narrow down one level at a time.
Why this stays fast at any scale. With a branching factor in the hundreds, tree depth grows as O(log n) — a four-level tree of 4KB pages with a branching factor of 500 can hold up to 256 TB, and a lookup still only touches four pages.

Growing the tree: what a page split looks like

Updating an existing key just overwrites its leaf page in place — cheap, and every reference to that page stays valid. Inserting a new key is more involved if the target page is already full: it splits into two half-full pages, and the parent gets updated to reflect the new boundary.

A full leaf page, about to receive one more key than it can hold.
Why this is dangerous without a write-ahead log. A split touches multiple pages at once — the two new half-pages, and the parent's references. A crash partway through leaves an orphaned page nothing points to. That's exactly why B-tree implementations log every change to a WAL before ever touching the real pages.

Reliability and optimizations, in short

Every B-tree write is really a write-ahead-log entry plus the actual page overwrite — that double-write is the seed of "write amplification," covered in more depth on the next page. Concurrent access needs latches (lightweight locks) protecting the tree's shared structure, since — unlike log-structured merging — updates happen in place, in the foreground, where other threads can see a tree mid-change.

Optimizations worth knowing: copy-on-write (write a modified page to a new location instead of overwriting, used by LMDB — sidesteps the WAL entirely); abbreviated keys in interior pages to raise the branching factor; keeping leaf pages roughly sequential on disk for faster range scans; sibling pointers between leaf pages so range scans don't need to climb back to the parent; and fractal trees, a variant borrowing log-structured ideas to cut disk seeks.
