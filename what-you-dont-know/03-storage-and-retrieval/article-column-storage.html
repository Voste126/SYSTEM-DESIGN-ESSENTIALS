chapter 03, part 3 — page 5 of 5 — built from zero

Column-oriented storage

If you've never met this idea before, start at step 0 and don't skip ahead — each step only makes sense once the one before it has landed.

step 0 — the problem

A 100-column table, a 3-column question

A warehouse fact table can easily have 100+ columns. A typical analytics question — "how much fruit and candy sold, by day of week, in 2013" — only actually needs three of them: the date, the product category, and the quantity. Every other column is dead weight for this one question.

A fact table with 7 sample columns (real ones have 100+). Press play.

step 1 — why row storage is the wrong shape here

Row-oriented vs. column-oriented reads

Row storage keeps one row's values physically together — perfect for "fetch this whole order," wasteful for "read three columns across a million rows." Column storage keeps one column's values together instead.

Same query, same data — watch what actually gets pulled off disk.

step 2 — how columns compress: bitmap encoding

Turning a column into bitwise-AND/OR-able bitmaps

Few distinct values, many rows — exactly the shape that compresses well. One bitmap per distinct value; a 1 marks which rows have it.

Column of product IDs across 12 rows. Press play.
Why this is fast, not just compact. "product IN (30, 68)" becomes a bitwise OR of two bitmaps. "product = 31 AND store = 3" becomes a bitwise AND of two bitmaps — cheap CPU operations, correct only because every column's bitmap lines up row-for-row.

step 3 — sort order multiplies the compression

The same column, before and after sorting

You can't sort each column independently — you'd lose which values belong to the same row. Sort whole rows together, by a chosen key, and low-cardinality columns end up with long unbroken runs that compress dramatically further.

An unsorted date column, about to be sorted.

step 4 — writes are the trade-off

Why this makes writing harder (and the fix)

You can't update a compressed, sorted column file in place — inserting one row could mean rewriting entire files. The fix is the same LSM-tree idea from earlier in this chapter: buffer writes in memory, merge them into the on-disk column files in bulk later. Queries transparently check both.

step 5 — pre-computing the aggregate itself

Data cubes: caching the answer, not just the data

If "total sales by date and product" gets asked constantly, why recompute it every time? A data cube precomputes every date-product combination once.

Two dimensions: date (rows) and product (columns). Press play.
The catch. This is blazingly fast for exactly the dimensions it was built with — but if "price bracket" was never one of the cube's dimensions, no amount of slicing the existing cube can answer "what fraction of sales was over $100." That's why warehouses keep the raw fact table too, and treat cubes as a targeted boost, not a replacement.
