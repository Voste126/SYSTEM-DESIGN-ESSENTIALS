senior engineer track — chapter 01

Reliable, scalable, maintainable

Three words that get thrown around loosely. This is what they mean precisely, and why the "obviously correct" architecture is sometimes wrong for your actual load.

01

A fault is not a failure

Reliability means the system keeps working correctly even in the face of hardware faults, software bugs, and human error. The important distinction: a fault is one component deviating from its spec; a failure is the whole system letting the user down. Good fault-tolerant design is entirely about the gap between those two — stopping faults from ever becoming failures, rather than pretending faults won't happen.

Hardware faults are usually random and independent — one disk dying doesn't predict another. Software faults are systematic and correlated across nodes, which is why they cause disproportionately more outages. Human error was found to be the leading cause of outages in one large study of internet services — ahead of hardware.

Counterintuitively, some of the most reliable systems deliberately increase the rate of faults — Netflix's Chaos Monkey randomly kills processes in production — because it's the only way to keep fault-tolerance machinery genuinely exercised instead of untested and rotting.

02

Scalability is a question, not a label

"Is X scalable" is close to a meaningless question on its own. The real question is: if load grows in a specific way, what are your options? Answering that requires describing load with real numbers first — requests/sec, read:write ratio, whatever's actually the bottleneck for your system.

Fan-out on read: posting is a single insert. Reading a timeline means joining across every followee at request time — cheap to write, expensive to read, and this is what Twitter outgrew first.

Percentiles beat averages. The mean response time hides how many users actually experienced the slow case. p50, p95, p99 tell you what fraction of requests were faster than a threshold — and high percentiles (tail latency) matter because a single slow backend call can make an entire multi-call request slow, even if only 1% of calls are ever slow individually.

03

Maintainability is where most of the cost lives

Most of a system's lifetime cost isn't building it — it's everything after: fixing bugs, adapting it, keeping it running. Three design principles make that cost lower: operability (can ops keep it healthy), simplicity (can a new engineer understand it), and evolvability (can it change as requirements shift).

Accidental vs. inherent complexity. Inherent complexity comes from the actual problem. Accidental complexity comes only from how you happened to implement it — and it's removable. Abstraction is the main tool: SQL hides on-disk structures and concurrency the same way a high-level language hides machine code.

None of these three properties are free lunches, and none are checklist items — they're lenses you keep applying as the system's actual load and requirements turn out to be different from what you assumed at design time.
