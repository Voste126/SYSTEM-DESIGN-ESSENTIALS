big tech track — chapter 01

What is software engineering?

"Software engineering is programming integrated over time." Drag the slider — the practices that make sense change completely depending on how long the code needs to survive.

01

Programming integrated over time

Programming is writing code that works right now. Software engineering is everything required to keep that code working — and keep it changeable — for as long as it actually needs to survive. Three things separate the two: time (will this need to survive an OS or language upgrade?), scale (does it need policies that still work at 10x the team size?), and trade-offs (constant cost-weighing with incomplete information).

Sustainability, precisely defined: your project is sustainable if, for its expected lifespan, you're capable of reacting to any valuable change — technical or business. Capable, not obligated. Being incapable is a silent bet that nothing important will ever need to change.

02

Hyrum's Law

"With a sufficient number of users of an API, it does not matter what you promise in the contract: all observable behaviors of your system will be depended on by somebody."

Nothing in a hash set's contract promises a particular iteration order. Doesn't matter — given enough users and enough time, someone will write code depending on whatever order it happens to produce today, maybe even using it as an ad hoc random number source. The undocumented behavior gets depended on anyway.

The practical takeaway isn't "never let anything be observable" — that's impossible. It's that every change to a widely-used system has to weigh not just "does this violate the documented contract" but "what real, undocumented behavior might people already be relying on."

03

Policies that scale, and ones that quietly don't

The test: imagine the org 10x or 100x bigger. Does the work required per engineer grow along with it? If yes, that's a scaling problem, regardless of how reasonable the policy sounds today.

  • Old-style deprecation ("we delete the old Widget on August 15th, migrate before then") pushes cost onto every downstream team — cost that grows with the dependency graph, not the size of the team doing the deprecating.
  • The Churn Rule fixes it: whoever owns the change does the migration themselves, or makes it backward-compatible in place. A few experts doing a well-understood migration beats every downstream team re-learning the problem from scratch.
  • The Beyoncé Rule — "if it broke and there was no CI test for it, that's not the fault of the change" — means the CI system, not manual tracking-down of every bespoke dependency, is the source of truth for "did this break anything." That scales flat regardless of headcount.
  • Long-lived dev branches work fine at 5-10 branches; the resync cost on every merge grows with the number of branches, which grows with team size — a structural reason trunk-based development tends to win at scale.

04

Shifting left

The developer workflow is a timeline: design → implementation → review → testing → commit → canary → production. The same bug costs more to fix the further right it's found — caught before commit, it's a quick fix by someone who already understands the feature; caught in production, it's an incident that needs someone else to investigate, triage, and remediate live.

05

Trade-offs and six kinds of cost

Financial, resource (compute), personnel (engineer time), transaction (cost of acting), opportunity (cost of not acting), and societal (impact on users, magnified at billion-user scale). Two examples make the "small decision, real trade-off" point concrete:

Whiteboard markers. Locking up a $1 marker to prevent hoarding trades away friction-free brainstorming to save almost nothing — a deliberately lopsided trade-off Google chose not to make, once actually priced out.
Distributed builds. Moving off local builds clearly saved engineer-time — but once individual engineers stopped feeling the pain of a bloated build directly, nobody was incentivized to keep dependencies lean, and bloat crept back in. Efficiency gains can increase total consumption, not just efficiency (Jevons Paradox).
